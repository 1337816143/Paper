Abstract
Crop diversification could enhance economic and environmental sustainability of crop farming, but achieving this requires 
careful planning that accounts for crop interrelationships in a rotation or spatial layout. Several models have been devised to 
design diversified crop rotations or crop spatial layouts, but they often fail to capture farm settings, production constraints, 
and sustainability objectives specific to a farm at both temporal and spatial scales. To fill this gap, we developed a novel 
rule-based whole-farm planning model, FarmSTEPS, for systematically exploring diversified and sustainable farm crop 
sequence plans in a spatial-temporally explicit manner. FarmSTEPS comprises multiple farm characterization modules, 
an algorithm for generating farm crop sequence plans, and a multi-attribute decision-aid tool. Users first characterize farm 
configurations and plot properties, and parameterize candidate crops according to the actual situation of farms. Subsequently 
the algorithm generates and evaluates all feasible crop sequence plans for each plot from the first planning period until the 
end of the planning window, subject to user-defined sequence- and farm-level constraints. Eventually, the model suggests a 
set of superior farm plans using the multi-attribute decision-aid tool. We illustrate the model with a Chinese wheat-maize 
farm to explore future three-year cropping plans targeting five sustainability objectives: maximizing gross margin and mini-
mizing four environmental impacts (groundwater depletion, pesticide input, aquatic eutrophication, GHG emission). The 
model generated 213,679 feasible plans, with 128 being Pareto-optimal in terms of the five objectives. Overall, relative to 
the wheat-maize baseline, these optimal plans increased gross margin by 3-50% while mitigating environmental impacts by 
10-56%, largely through the inclusion of tuber and oil crops, though trade-offs existed between economic and environmental 
benefits. The case study demonstrates that FarmSTEPS is an effective tool for designing diversified and sustainable crop 
sequence plans tailored to farm-specific conditions while informing stakeholders on potential benefits and trade-offs among 
sustainability objectives.

1  Introduction

Sustainable crop farming is required to produce sufficient 
crop products while ensuring farm incomes and minimiz-
ing environmental externalities (Cui et al. 2018). Many 
major crop-producing regions around the world have been 
experiencing crop specialization and intensification in the 
past few decades, with farmlands dominated by single or a 
few crop species, particularly major cereals (Ramankutty 
et al. 2018). This trend contributed to increased agricultural 
productivity in terms of dietary energy but caused various

farming sustainability challenges in both the economic and 
environmental domains, such as undesired and unstable 
operating profits, overexploitation of freshwater (particularly 
groundwater), exposure of humans and the environment to 
agrochemicals, greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, and agro-
biodiversity loss (Campbell et al. 2017; Chakraborti et al. 
2023), threatening economic well-being and environmental 
health. It is therefore crucial to transform currently intensi-
fied and specialized crop farming systems toward increased 
sustainability.
Crop diversification is deemed an effective strategy to 
improve the multi-attribute sustainability of crop farming 
(Hufnagel et al. 2020). From a temporal perspective, it

presents itself in the form of diversified crop sequences, i.e., 
cultivating a sequence of different crops on the same piece 
of land, while from a spatial perspective, crop diversification 
results in spatial cropping plans with different crops across 
different pieces of land every year (Dury et al. 2012). Sus-
tainability benefits of crop diversification have been found 
in many previous studies. A meta-analysis and a system-
atic review both indicated that diversified cropping systems 
could deliver higher financial returns relative to simplified 
systems, often via introducing high-profit crops such as veg-
etables and root crops (Sánchez et al. 2022; Feliciano 2019). 
Furthermore, multiple empirical and meta-analysis studies 
have demonstrated that diversification could reduce the reli-
ance on agricultural resource inputs, including groundwater, 
fertilizer, pesticide, and fossil energy, and mitigate associ-
ated environmental impacts by incorporating resource-effi-
cient crops such as beans, coarse cereals, and some oil crops 
(Boeraeve et al. 2020; Hunt et al. 2020; Guinet et al. 2023; 
Yang et al. 2024). The successful cases in these studies high-
light that achieving the sustainability benefits of diversified 
systems requires careful, reasoned planning.
Models serve as an efficient tool for planning sustain-
able and diversified crop production systems because they 
can handle, systematically and transparently, many factors 
for cropping system innovation, including various candi-
date crops, system constraints, and sustainability objectives 
(Dury et al. 2012). Several models have been devised to 
facilitate the planning of diversified systems either tempo-
rally or spatially. Among them, crop rotation generator mod-
els such as ROTAT+ and ROTOR can create all agronomi-
cally viable crop rotations at the plot scale and evaluate their 
multi-attribute sustainability, given a list of candidate crops 
and rotation rules (Dogliotti et al. 2003, 2004; Bachinger 
and Zander 2007). However, the generated sustainable rota-
tions are often difficult to implement on real farms since the 
models do not account for the farm-specific situations and 
demands, for example, the heterogeneity in size and proper-
ties of plots within a farm, resource accessibility and avail-
ability at a farm, and the whole-farm performance objectives 
pursued by farmers and other stakeholders. Moreover, these 
models were built merely for fixed rotation systems, which 
follow a cyclical pattern of component crops, rather than 
generating flexible and non-cyclical crop sequences for the 
future (Castellazzi et al. 2008). This model limitation might 
constrain the scope of sustainability improvement delivered 
by temporal crop diversification for farms (Dury et al. 2013).
Some whole-farm planning models are able to allocate 
crops or crop sequences to different farm plots to improve 
farm-level sustainability objectives using optimization tech-
niques such as linear programming and heuristic algorithms 
(Dogliotti et al. 2005; Groot et al. 2012; Sempore et al. 2015; 
Capitanescu et al. 2017). But there are several limitations 
to these models: 1) primarily optimizing a single objective

(usually profits) rather than multiple ones when using lin-
ear programming; 2) overlooking key agronomic constraints 
such as disallowed crop successions or maximum planting 
frequency of crops in a crop sequence; 3) missing period-
by-period assessment and constraints on farm cropping 
plans, e.g., the minimum farm profit in each year, making 
the resulting diversified farm plans less practical for farmers 
(Kaim et al. 2018); and 4) lacking capabilities to create non-
cyclical and flexible crop sequences over planning horizons 
(Janssen and Van Ittersum 2007). There is still a need for a 
modelling framework for the spatial-temporal exploration of 
whole-farm cropping plans based on farm-specific situations 
and multiple sustainability objectives. This topic remains 
largely unexplored.
The North China Plain is a major crop-producing region 
in China and a typical intensified and specialized crop farm-
ing region   , where over 60% of the farmland is currently 
devoted to annual double cropping of winter wheat and 
summer maize (Li and Lei 2021). This cereal-dominated 
system is highly water- and nutrient-demanding, with an 
estimated blue water demand of 300 mm/year and nitro-
gen uptake of 400 kg N/ha/year (Yang et al. 2021; Zhang 
2021). To achieve higher productivity, farmers often use a 
considerable amount of groundwater and fertilizers (Liang 
et al. 2022). This practice leads to a sharp groundwater table 
decline and a high N surplus (approximately 250 kg N/ha/
year; Zhang 2021), which contributes to waterbody eutrophi-
cation (Kong et al. 2016; Zhang et al. 2017). Additionally, 
Chinese agri-environmental policies target the reduction of 
chemical pesticides and the mitigation of carbon emissions 
(MARA 2021). To reduce the environmental burden aris-
ing from crop production, the local government encourages 
farmers to partially replace staple cereals with low-input 
crops, especially on large-scale farms that have sufficient 
resources and skills to manage new crops (Xu et al. 2024; 
Fig. 1). However, farm managers are more concerned about 
farm gross margin and lack methods for designing profitable 
yet environmentally benign farm cropping plans tailored to 
specific situations of their farms. With this need, the typi-
cal wheat-maize farm in the North China Plain could serve 
as an ideal case for model-based exploration of sustainable 
whole-farm crop sequence plans.
The primary aim of this study is to present a novel 
model, FarmSTEPS, for exploration of diversified and sus-
tainable crop sequence plans for crop farms aligned with 
farm-specific conditions and requirements. FarmSTEPS is 
a whole-farm modelling framework capable of systemati-
cally generating desired farm cropping plans in a spatial-
temporally explicit manner by integrating farm characteri-
zation modules, an exhaustive whole-farm crop sequence 
planning algorithm, and a multi-attribute decision-aid tool. 
The second aim is to illustrate the application of this model 
by exploring alternative options for a wheat-maize family

2  Model

We developed a rule-based model, FarmSTEPS (Spatio-
Temporal Exploration ProcedureS for crop allocation on 
a Farm, Fig. 2) to systematically generate and evaluate 
feasible crop sequence plans for plots within a given crop 
farm in a spatial-temporally explicit manner, subject to both 
sequence- and farm-associated constraints. This was done by 
integrating multiple modules for farm characterization and 
stakeholder expectations (section 2.1) with a whole-farm 
crop sequence planning algorithm (section 2.2). Model users 
could apply FarmSTEPS by parameterizing these modules 
in terms of the real situation and objectives and constraints

of the focus farm and accordingly obtain a set of possible 
cropping plans and their performance as model output. Then 
a multi-attribute decision-aid tool, Pareto ranking, efficiently 
identifies the superior solutions from the entire set of pos-
sible plans in terms of sustainability objectives of interests, 
which could inform the stakeholders’ decision-making pro-
cess (Section 2.3). We programmed the model software in 
the C# language in Microsoft Visual Studio.

2.1  FarmSTEPS modules

2.1.1  Farm configuration module

Since each plot within a farm is the basic unit for crop 
sequence planning, the initial step in applying the model 
is configuring the farm by loading the farm plot map in an 
ESRI shape file, where each plot is represented by a GIS 
polygon. The key aim of this module is to read, store, and 
display the farm plot configuration along with relevant geo-
graphical attributes, e.g., the position, size, and slope per 
plot.

2.1.2  Land‑unit module

Each plot should be linked with a specific land-unit, which 
describes characteristics of the plot such as soil properties 
and includes a list of candidate crops that can be grown 
on the plot. This allows to consider the differences in envi-
ronmental conditions, crop selection, and crop performance 
among plots in a farm. For example, some of the plots might 
not have access to irrigation and therefore some drought-
sensitive crops are banned and the candidate crop yields in 
these plots would be lower than those in irrigated plots. The 
land-units are set in the ROTAT+ software that is connected

to FarmSTEPS. ROTAT+ is normally used as a tool for 
field-level crop rotation generation (Liang et al. 2023) while 
acting as a module in FarmSTEPS to record the land and 
crop information. Here, users need to parameterize each 
candidate crop with information that is essential for gener-
ating whole-farm crop sequence plans and calculating their 
indicator performance (see Section 2.1.5), including plant-
ing and harvesting dates, agricultural resource inputs (e.g., 
labor requirement, fertilizer application, pesticide input and 
irrigation water consumption), and crop and pollutant out-
put (e.g., yield, revenue, nutrient losses, GHG emissions).

2.1.3  Timeline module

To reflect the actual farmers’ dynamic decision-making pro-
cesses, several timeline parameters should be set for farm 
planning. The first one is the starting date of planning, deter-
mined based on when farmers would change the current crop 
arrangement or the start of the cropping season. Secondly, 
the length of the planning period, reflecting how often farm-
ers assess farm performance and make cropping decisions, 
must be defined. One or multiple crops can be sown and 
harvested per planning period. This period typically spans 
one year but can vary, ranging from shorter (e.g., one quar-
ter) to longer (e.g., multiple years). The third parameter is 
the number of planning periods for crop sequence genera-
tion, also known as the “planning window” in FarmSTEPS. 
Additionally, users can specify the length of historical crop 
sequences prior to the starting date of farm planning, ena-
bling the model to account for the cropping history of each 
farm plot. All of these timeline parameters are applied uni-
formly across all plots within a farm.

2.1.4  Crop sequence module

With defined timeline parameters, this module allows to set 
the historical and baseline crop sequences for each plot in 
a farm. Setting historical crop sequences incorporates the 
influence of past crops on future crop planning in the model. 
Baseline crop sequences represent the farmers’ original idea 
for arranging crop sequences across plots in the planning 
window, providing a reference for comparing with novel 
plans. Users can choose a few candidate crops from the land-
units to constitute historical and baseline crop sequences 
for plots.

2.1.5  Indicator module

Indicator assessment in FarmSTEPS relies on crop input-
output relationships so model users can define any indicators 
of their interest that are calculated in terms of crop input-
output dataset to assess farm cropping plans. For instance, 
dietary nutrient yield from the human-nutrition domain, 
gross margin and labor use from the socio-economic 
domain, and groundwater use and GHG emission from the 
environmental domain. These indicators can be set as objec-
tives for improving sustainability benefits or as constraints 
for eliminating undesired solutions during planning.
For a given whole-farm crop sequence plan, the module 
begins with evaluating the crop-level performance ­(Ii,p,t,c), 
i.e., the ith indicator performance of the component crop c

of the sequence on the plot p in the tth planning period. If 
the growing season of a crop spans two planning periods,

between periods proportionate to the growing duration or 
credited to the period when the crop is harvested. The former 
is suitable for indicators associated with agricultural inputs 
such as labor input and environmental costs while the latter

2.1.6  Constraint module

ensure that the generated farm cropping plans comply with 
the agronomic rules and satisfy the farm-specific demands. 
Here, all constraints are classified into two types: longitudi-

are applied to crop sequences on each plot while transversal 
ones are applied to the whole farm.

context: the maximum planting frequency of a certain crop 
(group), the minimum interval between repeated planting 
of a certain crop (group), and the disallowed crop suc-

ultimately provides all feasible farm-plans with their cor-

Specifically speaking, the planning commences with the 
generation of feasible crop sequences per plot in the first 
planning period. For each plot in the farm, the algorithm 
constructs a crop-sequence containing a list of crops that 
have been grown before the start of the first planning-period, 
i.e. the plot history. Then the last day of the historical crop-
sequence is checked to determine whether there is growing 
time to accommodate a one or more crops in the first plan-
ning-period. If growing time is available, the crop-sequence 
adds the first crop or successive crops from the candidate 
crop list of the plot. Then the crop sequence rules are applied 
to the crop-sequence to examine the agronomic feasibil-
ity. If this crop-sequence conforms to agronomic rules, it 
is stored in the collection of crop-sequences for the plot. 
After that, the added crop(s) in the crop-sequence is(are) 
emptied to add and examine the next single crop and suc-
cessive crops from the list. This process runs throughout all 
candidate crops for the plot and then generates a collection 
of all agronomically feasible crop-sequences in each plot in 
the first planning-period.
Subsequently, the algorithm produces all feasible farm 
cropping plans during the first planning-period. In doing so, 
a farm-plan by combining feasible crop-sequences across 
all plots in the farm within the first planning-period. If this 
farm-plan does not violate the period-level constraints, it is 
added to the collection of farm-plans for the first planning-
period. Similarly, the farm-plan deletes the first combina-
tion, and the above process is then repeated to the rest of 
all combinations of crop-sequences to generate the whole 
collection of feasible farm-plans. Moreover, a crop-sequence 
would be marked if used in a feasible farm plan. The crop-
sequences that are not marked at the end of the farm plan 
generation are eliminated from the collection of feasible 
crop-sequences for a plot.

sequences and farm-plans is also applied for the each of 
the following planning-periods until the end of the plan-
ning window. Here, a crop-sequence for a plot builds on not 
only its historical sequence but also on one feasible sequence

2.3  Identification of Pareto‑optimal farm plans

alternative farm-plans, some of which may underperform in 
terms of sustainability objectives. These suboptimal farm-
plans could therefore be excluded from those recommended 
to end users. Here, we applied the Pareto ranking method, 
a non-weighing multi-criteria decision-aid tool, to identify 
the Pareto-optimal plans from the entire set of generated 
farm-plans. A plan was ranked as Pareto-optimal when its 
performance of any one objective cannot be outstripped by 
another farm-plan without these other plans being inferior to

3  Case study

3.1  Case farm description

The case farm modeled in FarmSTEPS is a single farm 
located in Quzhou County, Hebei Province, China 
(114°98′E, 36°94′N, Fig. 4). This farm is a large-scale family 
farm with a cropland area of 23 ha. Quzhou has a temperate 
continental monsoon climate, with a mean annual tempera-
ture of 13.1 ℃ and a mean annual precipitation of 430 mm. 
The farm has a loamy textured calcareous alluvial soil, with 
a soil bulk density of 1.43 g/cm3, a pH of 8.2, and a soil 
organic matter content of 1.57%. Farm managers have been 
consecutively cultivating only wheat-maize double cropping 
on the whole farm for at least five years. Double cropping 
of wheat and maize is a common cropping pattern in the 
North China Plain due to its high output of cereal grain for 
human consumption (wheat) and farm animals (maize) and 
the availability of mechanization.
We selected this farm as a case study to illustrate the 
capabilities of FarmSTEPS for both practical and methodo-
logical reasons.
From a practical perspective, the farmers are currently 
unsatisfied with the farming profitability and therefore seek 
to improve farm gross margin by diversifying cropping plans. 
Furthermore, the local government endeavors to reduce 
resource and environmental pressures arising from agricultural 
production such as groundwater overdraft, nutrient pollution, 
pesticide overuse, and GHG emissions by collaborating with 
large-scale farms that can set an example for nearby, often 
smaller, farms. The case farm also encounters practical con-
straints related to labor availability, market outlets, and on-
farm management in the face of altering cropping plans. Thus, 
the farm acts as a suitable case to use FarmSTEPS to explore 
diversified farm crop sequence plans towards achieving multi-
attribute farming sustainability.

From a methodological perspective, this farm represents 
multiple dimensions of complexity relevant to whole-farm 
crop planning, specifically:
Variable plot size: Although the farm consists of only five 
plots, their sizes vary considerably. This heterogeneity in plot 
area markedly influences crop allocation decisions and farm-
level outcomes;
Diverse candidate crops: The crop portfolio includes a 
wide range of crops, from staple cereals and tuber crops to oil 
crops and vegetables, with distinct crop calendars, agronomic 
requirements, and economic and environmental profiles;

1)	 Land-unit-specific crop set: The farm includes two 
land units, each with a different set of candidate crops, 
reflecting spatial heterogeneity in farm management.
2)	 Flexible cropping intensity: The one-year unit planning 
period allows for both single and double cropping sys-
tems, increasing the number of feasible crop sequence 
combinations.
3)	 Multi-objective assessment: Five sustainability indica-
tors (economic returns, groundwater depletion, pesticide 
input, eutrophication potential, and GHG emissions) 
were used to evaluate the cropping plans, capturing 
trade-offs between farm profitability and environmental 
impacts.
4)	 Complex constraints: In addition to crop sequence rules, 
we considered transversal constraints at the period and 
farm levels. These include limits on total production and 
harvested area of crops, as well as thresholds for dietary 
energy yield, and socioeconomic and environmental 
indicators.

This case study does not address certain dimensions of 
complexity, such as spatial variability in soil across plots 
(Aggarwal et al. 2022) or a longer planning horizon (> three 
years), which could be tested in the future studies. However, 
given the multiple forms of complexity represented above, this 
case provides a robust and sufficiently representative testbed 
for illustrating the core capabilities of FarmSTEPS.

4  Results

4.1  Farm baseline plan versus Pareto‑optimal plans

The baseline crop sequence plan for the case farm, i.e., con-
tinuous wheat-maize double cropping over future three years 
across all plots, resulted in a farm gross margin of 2303 
USD/ha/yr, groundwater depletion of 284 mm/yr, pesticide 
input of 6.67 kg AI/ha/yr, eutrophication potential of 28.9 
kg ­PO4 eq./ha/yr, and GHG emission of 10.0 Mg ­CO2 eq./
ha/yr (Fig. 5).
FarmSTEPS generated 213,679 feasible farm-plans, with 
128 of them being Pareto-optimal in terms of five sustain-
ability objectives. Compared with the baseline farm plan, 
these Pareto-optimal farm-plans achieved 3–50% higher 
farm gross margin while reaching 32–56% lower groundwa-
ter depletion, 31–47% lower pesticide input, 10–33% lower 
eutrophication potential and 22–40% lower GHG emission. 
Thus, the diversification of crop sequences on the case farm 
resulted in improvements of all five indicators, though trade-
offs existed between economic and environmental objectives 
(Fig. 5).
The improved sustainability performance of optimal 
farm-plans was mainly due to the inclusion of non-cereal 
crops, particularly sweet potato and sunflower (Table 2). 
On the largest plot in the farm, Plot 4, half of the optimal 
farm-plans suggested a sweet potato-based crop-sequence 
(SwPotato-Wheat-SuMaize-SwPotato), and the other half 
combined sweet potato and sunflower with wheat-maize 
double cropping. On the intermediate-size plots, Plot 2 and 
4, most generated crop-sequences were still combinations 
of sweet potato, sunflower, and wheat-maize double crop-
ping in the crop sequence, accounting for 37% and 64% of 
optimal plans for two plots respectively. However, the model 
generated garlic-maize double cropping for Plot 2, a plot in 
which labor-intensive crops were allowed to be planted, in 
36% of optimal plans. The generated crop-sequences on Plot 
1, the smallest plot, offered more space for crop diversifica-
tion across optimal plans, with only 5% involving wheat-
maize double cropping.

4.2  Example farm plans

We selected and presented three individual plans as exam-
ples from the Pareto-optimal set of 128 farm-plans (Fig. 6). 
These three example farm-plans showed contrasting sustain-
ability profiles, including one profit-prioritized farm plan,

one environment-prioritized plan, and one plan that achieved 
a balance between profitability and environmental soundness 
(Fig. 5; Fig. 6).
The profit-prioritized farm-plan created the highest farm 
gross margin, 50% higher than the baseline, while also 
incurring the highest environmental costs across optimal 
plans, but still below the baseline (Fig. 5; Fig. 6). Such 
performance resulted from the crop-sequence on the larg-
est plot (Plot 4) being dominated by sweet potato, the most 
profitable crop in this plot, as well as crop-sequences that 
frequently cultivated profitable crops, especially garlic, on 
the intermediate- and smallest-size plots (Plot 1–2, Fig. 6). 
In contrast, the environment-prioritized plan was one of the 
least environmentally impactful plans across the Pareto set 
but also the third least profitable one. Compared with the 
baseline, this plan reduced environmental costs by 32–56% 
while increasing gross margin by only 9%. The success of 
its environmental performance was in part due to the culti-
vation of sunflower, the most resource-saving crop, and the 
markedly decreased land-use intensity realized by replacing 
wheat-maize double cropping with low-input single crops 
in a year, such as sweet potato, peanut, and spring maize 
(Fig. 6).
The balanced farm-plan resulted in an intermediate level 
of sustainability performance between the profit- and envi-
ronment-prioritized farm-plan. Similar to the profit-prior-
itized farm-plan, its crop sequence in Plot 4 still included 
sweet potato, while the crop-sequences on the intermediate- 
and smallest-size plot presented a mix of profitable crops, 
e.g., garlic and potato, and less resource-intensive crops, 
e.g., sunflower and sweet potato.

5  Discussion

In this study, we presented a novel rule-based model, 
FarmSTEPS, for exploring sustainable and diversified 
farm crop sequence plans aligned with farm-specific situa-
tions and constraints. This modelling framework integrates 
farm characterization modules, an algorithm for exhaus-
tive whole-farm crop sequence planning, and a multi-
attribute decision-aid tool that can recommend a set of 
desirable farm cropping plans in a systematic and spatial-
temporally explicit manner. We applied FarmSTEPS in a 
case farm and successfully obtained 128 optimal three-
year crop sequence plans, which markedly outperformed 
the baseline yearly repeating wheat-maize double cropping 
plan in gross margin, groundwater depletion, pesticide 
input, eutrophication potential, and GHG emission concur-
rently, albeit with trade-offs occurring between economic 
and environmental objectives. The plot-specific crop 
sequences of these optimal plans highlighted the impor-
tant role of a few of candidate crops, e.g., sweet potato

the model outcomes do not only present the potential and

promising crops and crop sequences for the studied farm, 
which could support the stakeholders’ discussion to facili-

choices. While demonstrated on a single case farm, the 
FarmSTEPS model serves as a flexible framework that 
can be adapted to diverse crop farming contexts by adjust-

constraints and objectives, thus supporting the navigation

of sustainable cropping solutions tailored to different farm 
circumstances and aspirations.

The design of sustainable and diversified crop sequence 
plans for farms is a complex process, entailing a combina-
tion of many elements: plot configuration and heterogeneity, 
various candidate crops, various sequence- and farm-level 
constraints, numerous decision-making periods, and mul-
tiple sustainability goals. We developed the FarmSTEPS 
model to assist farm stakeholders to systematically generate

Many of existing whole-farm planning models, such as 
FarmIMAGES (Dogliotti et al. 2005) and FarmDESIGN 
(Groot et al. 2012), also aim to address these challenges, 
albeit through different methodological approaches. 
FarmIMAGES identifies more sustainable cropping plans 
by first generating multi-year crop rotations at the plot 
scale, followed by a multi-objective mixed-integer pro-
gramming algorithm to allocate these rotations across fixed 
plots (Dogliotti et al. 2014). This structure enables the 
efficient selection of a single optimal plan that enhances 
mean annual economic and environmental performance 
at the whole-farm level. While this approach is computa-
tionally efficient and robust, its single-solution outcome 
may overlook alternative plans that align with diverse 
stakeholder priorities. In contrast, FarmDESIGN uses a 
heuristic algorithm to allocate crop rotations based on 
proportional farmland area, resulting in a set of near-opti-
mal solutions (Ditzler et al. 2019). Its flexible approach, 
allowing crop allocation without fixed plot boundaries, 
can reveal new opportunities for improving farm-scale 
sustainability outcomes. However, due to the nature of 
heuristic optimization, the solutions may be non-robust 
in some cases and may converge to local optima, espe-
cially when the number of crop rotation options is large. 
FarmSTEPS, by comparison, takes a comprehensive and

exhaustive approach: it first enumerates all agronomically 
feasible crop sequences at the plot level, and then system-
atically constructs all possible whole-farm plans across the 
planning horizon by combining these sequences over time 
and space, while incorporating spatial and temporal con-
straints. This integration of exhaustive enumeration with 
constraint-guided filtering allows the model to spatial-
temporally explicitly generate and preserve all promising 
plans while aligning them with real-world sustainability 
goals and stakeholder preferences. Through Pareto-based 
evaluation, FarmSTEPS effectively narrows the solution 
space and provides a set of desired farm plans against mul-
tiple sustainability objectives. Despite the heavier compu-
tational burden and limited flexibility in modifying plot 
structures within the model, these challenges can be fully 
or partially addressed by incorporating appropriate con-
straints and importing revised farm plot maps that reflect 
the desired configurations.
Compared with existing models, one of the primary 
advancements of FarmSTEPS is its ability to assess farm-
scale sustainability performance and enforce production 
constraints at finer temporal scales, such as on an annual 
basis within a multi-year planning horizon. For example, 
many models do not account for year-by-year gross mar-

competing objectives and are socially and institutionally 
acceptable (Liang et al. 2026). For example, group work-
shops can provide a platform for stakeholders to collectively 
evaluate the feasibility, benefits, and potential limitations of 
selected plans (Quinio et al. 2022). Such workshops could 
help identify and improve compromise solutions that cater 
for the demands of multiple actors and promote the transla-
tion of model outputs into actionable and context-sensitive 
decisions for implementation.

5.2  Opportunities for widespread application 
of the FarmSTEPS model

The FarmSTEPS model holds strong potential for broad 
application in exploring sustainable and diversified farm 
cropping plans across different world regions. Its flexibility 
in dealing with different farm settings, crop portfolios, pro-
duction constraints, and sustainability objectives makes it 
well-suited to a variety of agricultural contexts. The model 
can be adapted for use on farms that vary by crop type (from 
arable farms and horticultural farms, to grassland farms), 
production scale (from smallholder farms to large-scale 
farms), and management modes (from organic to industrial). 
However, successful application requires necessary adapta-
tions of model inputs to reflect specific farm conditions.
These adaptations fall into five main aspects:

1)	 Farm plot configurations: Users can extract plot map 
from satellite or aerial imagery and convert them into 
ESRI files that records geographical attributes of each 
plot such as location, area, and slope.

2)	 Sustainability objectives: Farm stakeholders may opt 
for varying sets of sustainability indicators to serve as 
objectives in cropping plan design. For instance, sub-
sistence smallholders may prioritize the production 
of dietary nutrients ranging from calorific energy and 
protein to minerals and vitamins (Timler et al. 2020), 
and farms in mountainous areas focus on lowering soil 
erosion (Smith et al. 2017). FarmSTEPS allows users to 
flexibly define or select any indicators computable from 
by crop and plot parameters within defined land-units.
3)	 Land-units and candidate crops: Users can define the 
land-units for each of farm plots, where soil proper-
ties, resource availability, and candidate crops for each 
plot are specified according to the actual situation. The 
selection of candidate crops is guided by factors such as 
compatibility with pedo-climatic environments, market 
orientation, and farmers’ preferences (Adhikari et al. 
2023). For instance, horticultural farms may include 
only vegetables, fruits and flowers as candidates. Cru-
cially, users must collect crop data required for the 
assessment of farm-specific sustainability objectives. 
Such input-output data of candidate crops should reflect 
their performance under the farming context of the focus 
farm or plot. For example, for a particular crop, higher 
yield and water usage on irrigated plots versus rainfed 
plots, and lower yield and less synthetic agrochemical 
inputs in organic farms versus conventional farms. The 
input-output inventory of crops can be compiled based 
on a variety of sources, including local farm surveys, 
expert knowledge, statistical documents, and scientific 
literature (Liang et al. 2023).
4)	 Production constraints: The longitudinal and transversal 
constraints can be tailored to farm’s unique barriers and 
demands. Longitudinal constraints, i.e., crop sequence 
rules, may be formulated based on local agronomists and 
local field observations. Transversal constraints can be 
defined not only in terms of total requirement and har-
vested area of crops per unit planning period, but also 
input or indicator thresholds. For example, annual irri-
gation water quotas may apply in farms in water-scarce 
regions (Zhang and Oki 2023), while fertilizer quotas 
could be imposed for farms in nutrient-surplus regions 
(Expósito and Velasco 2020).
5)	 Planning timeline: Timeline parameters can be deter-
mined with farmers to reflect their actual decision-
making behaviors and account for the farm reality. 
For instance, the unit planning period may exceed one 
year to account for perennial crops on grassland farms. 
Organic farms, which often adopt long-term crop 
sequences, may benefit from planning windows that 
span a decade or more.

With above adaptations, our model can create a set of 
tailored cropping plans for individual farms, providing 
stakeholders with valuable insights into the potential ben-
efits and trade-offs across multiple sustainability objectives. 
Widespread adoption of the model among farms in a region 
could also help policymakers understand the contribution 
of crop diversification to combating regional sustainability 
challenges and the intractable trade-offs across objectives 
most farms perhaps encounter. This, in turn, can assist poli-
cymakers in identifying macro-level solutions, e.g., incentiv-
izing machinery specific to certain crops, to further foster 
agricultural sustainability.
Lastly, while the model is flexible and versatile, Farm-
STEPS is specifically designed for whole-farm crop 
sequence planning and does not currently account for other 
dimensions of farm management, such as livestock systems, 
by-product allocation, or landscape-level modifications.

5.3  Limitations

Several limitations of the model may need to be addressed 
in future work. First, we did not consider the uncertainty in 
crop yields, market prices, and weather over the planning 
window (Komarek et al. 2020), as the current version of 
FarmSTEPS is deterministic. In this case study, we used 
crop input-output data collected in a year with a normal 
weather pattern and average price data over the past five 
years to mitigate the effects of uncertainty. Moving forward, 
we may think of introducing stochastic parameters, allowing 
users to input a reasonable distribution of parameters rather 
than fixed values (Ballot et al. 2018). This will enable us to 
examine the effects of future uncertainty on the performance 
of whole-farm plans, though generating outcomes would fur-
ther aggravate the computational burden of obtaining the 
solutions and their performance.
Furthermore, FarmSTEPS did not account for crop inter-
actions at temporal and spatial scales. For example, the yield 
of a crop may be influenced by preceding crops in a crop 
sequence (Reckling et al. 2016), and farmers should avoid 
planting some crops in neighboring plots to prevent spread 
of pests and diseases and associated yield loss (Juventia 
et al. 2022). Incorporating pre-crop effects and crop topo-
logical constraints into the next version of FarmSTEPS will 
enhance the accuracy of farm assessments.
FarmSTEPS serves as a valuable tool for exploring trade-
offs across multiple sustainability objectives in the redesign 
of farm cropping plans. However, the current modeling 
framework does not yet incorporate quantitative trade-off 
analysis methods, such as sensitivity analysis and shadow 
price analysis (Kanter et al. 2018). Sensitivity analysis 
can help identify how small changes in model inputs (e.g., 
resource availability, crop yields, or market prices) influence 
outcomes across different objectives, thereby highlighting

which parameters most affect the trade-off dynamics. 
Shadow price analysis estimates the marginal cost or benefit 
of improving one objective at the expense of another, offer-
ing stakeholders a clearer picture of the ‘price’ of trade-offs 
(Breure et al. 2024). Integrating such methods into future 
applications of FarmSTEPS could provide a deeper under-
standing of the drivers and intensities of trade-offs, thereby 
enhancing transparency and supporting more informed, 
evidence-based decision-making.
Lastly, FarmSTEPS remains challenging for farmers to 
use independently. The first barrier is its application involves 
extensive data collection and specialized tasks such as farm 
mapping and multi-criteria assessment. Therefore, at this 
stage, agricultural advisors are crucial in working with farm-
ers to use the model and explore more sustainable farming 
options. Moreover, running the model can require high-per-
formance hardware in some cases. In our case study, a farm 
with five plots, eight candidate crops, and three planning 
periods required approximately 15 minutes for FarmSTEPS 
to generate all feasible farm plans. As the number of land 
parcels, alternative crops, and planning horizons increases, 
high-performance computing platforms may be needed 
to complete the crop planning process efficiently. In such 
situations, IT specialists are essential to support farmers in 
model implementation, computation management, and result 
interpretation.

6  Conclusion

In this study, we presented a novel farm planning model, 
FarmSTEPS, built to systematically explore sustainable and 
diversified crop sequence plans for crop farms in a spatial-
temporally explicit manner. The model can capture spatial 
configuration, plot heterogeneity, production constraints, and 
development objectives specific to each farm, thus ensur-
ing that generated plans are tailored to each farm’s unique 
conditions. We illustrated it using a wheat-maize farm in 
China, where the model created a set of optimal plans supe-
rior to the baseline plan in both economic and environmen-
tal performance, while also uncovering a trade-off between 
these two sustainability domains. FarmSTEPS can assist in 
identifying the critical crops and crop sequences specific to 
each plot for sustainable farm production. Users can cus-
tomize the model for farms of interest according on-farm 
realities, which offers opportunities for widespread applica-
tion of our model across different farming regions. Overall, 
FarmSTEPS serves as an efficient tool for designing sus-
tainable whole-farm cropping plans and informing stake-
holders about synergies and trade-offs among sustainability 
objectives.
