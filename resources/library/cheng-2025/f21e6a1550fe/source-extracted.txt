A B S T R A C T

CONTEXT: Intensive agriculture is under pressure from changing demands from society, prompting the need to
redesign agricultural landscapes to provide multiple ecosystem services (ESs). However, implementation of
changed practices requires positive engagement from stakeholders. Therefore, their perspective on ecosystem
services needs to be known.
OBJECTIVE: This study investigates stakeholders’ perspectives on multiple ESs in Quzhou County, an area in the
North China Plain used for intensified cereal production. We aim to elucidate perspectives within and across
diverse stakeholder groups (farmers, companies, citizens, academics, village and township heads, and county
government staff).
METHODS: Employing the Q methodology, we identified differences in perspectives within stakeholder groups
and we compared the similarities and differences of those perspectives across stakeholder groups. We also
investigated how farmers’ personal and household characteristics were related to the perspectives they held.
RESULTS AND CONCLUSIONS: Significant differences in preference emerged among stakeholder groups. Aca-
demics assigned higher importance to regulating and supporting services than other stakeholder groups and
companies assigned less importance to cultural services. We identified 18 distinct perspectives across seven

1. Introduction

Intensive agriculture needs transformation towards greater sustain-
ability worldwide, which may require a redesign of agricultural land-
scapes (Jackson et al., 2007; Landis, 2017). Agricultural landscapes
provide multiple ecosystem services (ESs) to a range of stakeholders
(O’Farrell and Anderson, 2010). An unbalanced ESs delivery leads to
compromised sustainability and biodiversity loss (Foley, 2005), poten-
tially leaving some people’s demand for ESs unmet. It is necessary to
assess the importance of ESs for stakeholders when adjusting the man-
agement of a landscape to rebalance the ESs supply. The importance of
ESs for stakeholders is strongly shaped by the socio-economic and cul-
tural context and by their (subjective) values, which mediate between
the benefits derived from services and the processes of governance that
steer agroecosystem management decisions (Breyne et al., 2021).
The value of ESs refers to economic (monetary) values, but also non-
monetary, socio-cultural values (MEA, 2005). Economic values high-
light the importance of certain ESs by assigning them high monetary
worth, which helps raise awareness and communicate their significance
to policymakers (de Groot et al., 2012). Socio-cultural values express
people’s opinions on the non-monetary importance of ESs and are re-
flected in stakeholder preferences for the multiple ESs that are provided
by an agroecosystem (see Table 1 for definitions). Insight into stake-
holder preferences can firstly aid policymakers in understanding how
changes in landscape management are perceived by different stake-
holders. Secondly, it informs decision-making in situations where trade-
offs among various (categories of) performance indicators occur
(Scholte et al., 2015). More inclusive and equitable decision-making is
facilitated by the integration of socio-cultural values with ecological and
monetary assessment. Contrasting perspectives on these diverse values
may exist and should be considered, in particular when stakeholders are
engaged in the assessment process (Martín-L´opez et al., 2012).
The degree to which the demand and supply of multiple ESs can be
balanced within a society or community depends on the heterogeneity of
demands, the potential changes that can be realized in the supply of the
ESs and the extent to which spatial distances or temporal lags hamper
the timely supply of demanded ESs (Mouchet et al., 2014). These con-
siderations should also inform decision-making and policy formulation
(Bulkeley and Mol, 2003). Here we focus only on the heterogeneity on
the demand side. Different stakeholders that are active or have an in-
terest in certain landscapes could have different preferences for ESs
depending on their roles, demographic characteristics, and other

attributes (Smith and Sullivan, 2014). While farmers might prefer pro-
visioning services for their well-being, urban visitors might show a
preference for cultural services (Zoderer et al., 2019), while regional
development programs and researchers would tend to assign greater
importance to regulating services to support the sustainable use of the
landscape (Peter et al., 2022). Within a certain stakeholder group, there
may also be different perspectives among individuals (Teixeira et al.,
2018). However, few studies have analyzed the heterogeneity of pref-
erences within stakeholder groups (Bernues et al., 2019).
Different methodologies could be applied to assess socio-cultural
values related to ESs, including expert-based approaches, focus group
discussions, interviews and questionnaire surveys (Chen et al., 2017;
Iniesta-Arandia et al., 2014; Lamarque et al., 2011; Page and Bellotti,
2015; Smith and Sullivan, 2014). However, as found in previous studies
and in our own pilot survey, when respondents are asked about the
importance of different ESs, nearly all respondents state that most ESs
are important (Horne et al., 2005). However, not all ESs can be provided
maximally at the same time. Trade-offs among ESs make it necessary to
prioritize ESs in decision-making and planning processes. Hence there is
a need for assessment approaches that give insight into the preferences
of ESs among stakeholders.
Q methodology is a semi-quantitative method for eliciting and sys-
tematically presenting stakeholder perspectives. It originates from psy-
chology and has also been used for many years in policy science and
public health (McKeown and Thomas, 2013, Watts and Stenner, 2005,
Valenta and Wigger, 1997). It aims to reveal the way individuals think
about a specific subject (Watts and Stenner, 2005) and assesses if groups
of individuals perceive a subject similarly (Stephenson, 1953). Con-
ducting Q methodology involves generating a set of statements that
represent the various facets of a discourse or problem, which are then
sorted by participants by the degree of agreement with the statements.
The sorting result is statistically analyzed to identify common sorting
patterns among participants (Watts and Stenner, 2005). In this paper, we
refer to typical sorting patterns as perspectives. Perspectives thus
represent typical patterns of how stakeholders think about the relative
importance of different ESs. Q sorting embedded in Q methodology
encourages participants to sort the items using deliberate thinking.
Q methodology has gradually gained credence in environmental and
agricultural science to assess the subjective values of stakeholders when
designing management and policymaking (Jiren et al., 2023; Rietveld
et al., 2023; Swagemakers et al., 2017; Timler et al., 2023; Vaas et al.,
2019; van Dijk et al., 2022). There are however only few applications of
Q methodology to capturing stakeholders’ perspectives on the impor-
tance of ESs in agricultural landscapes (Ciftcioglu, 2021) compared to
other ecosystems, e.g., nature reserves (Mahlalela et al., 2022), national
forests (Armatas et al., 2018), aquaculture (Pethiyagoda et al., 2024),
and urban parks (Buchel and Frantzeskaki, 2015).
The North China Plain is one of the largest crop production areas in
China and is characterized by simplified agricultural landscapes with a
dominance of wheat, maize and cotton (Xu et al., 2024). As a major
breadbasket of China, the region is geared towards enhancing provi-
sioning services (i.e. production of food, feed and other commodities),
and generation of economic value. Regions with intensive production
are often characterized by sustainability issues such as declining
groundwater tables (Zhao et al., 2017), greenhouse gas emissions (Zhao

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Case study area

We conducted our study in Quzhou county, located in Heibei Prov-
ince of the North China Plain (NCP). The surface area of Quzhou is
676.7 km2 including 525.2 km2 of cropland (NBSC, 2017). Winter
wheat, summer maize and cotton are the main crops, occupying together
approximately 80 % of the cropland area (NBSC, 2017). The landscapes
in Quzhou county are simplified and intensively used for agricultural
production, with mostly arable cropping and only a small area of non-
crop habitat, and 85.5 % of the population in Quzhou is rural (NBSC,
2017). As a representative area in the NCP, Quzhou has been selected as
a National Agriculture Green Development Pilot Demonstration Zone in
2019. Since 2020, the Quzhou government has formulated policies to
transform the county towards sustainable agricultural development in
the context of the Agriculture Green Development (AGD) program, which
is facilitated by Chinese national policies (Shen et al., 2020). We chose
Quzhou for this study because the area has sustainability issues, and is in
need of an agricultural green transition.
Quzhou county has 10 townships including 342 administrative vil-
lages. In this study, we selected 35 villages in Quzhou (Fig. S2(a)). Five
villages were sampled from each of 7 different village types, which
together cover the diversity of socio-economic conditions and agricul-
tural activity within the county (Xu et al., 2024). The sampling strategy
was equally weighted among the seven clusters to capture the full di-
versity of stakeholders’ views without regard for the size of each cluster
(number of villages in it). Details about the sampled villages and their
locations can be found in Cheng et al. (2023), Xu et al. (2024) and Fig.
S2.

2.2. Q methodology and data collection

An application of Q methodology proceeds in four steps: (1) research
design, (2) data collection, (3) analysis, and (4) interpretation (Zabala
et al., 2018). In step 1, the researcher selects a topic or issue to study and
describes the discourse around the topic and generates a set of state-
ments (Q set); this set represents different perspectives or opinions on
the topic. Here the statements refer to different ESs and the topic is the
importance of different ESs delivered by agricultural landscapes in the
NCP, and in Quzhou in particular (Section 2.2.1). The researcher then
recruits the participants (p set) who are identified as key stakeholders
and have knowledge or experience related to the topic (Section 2.2.2).
During step 2 (data collection), the researcher conducts the Q sorting,

which involves having participants rank the statements according to
their perceived importance (Section 2.2.3). Our Q sort distinguished five
levels of importance: very unimportant, unimportant, neutral, important
and very important (Fig. 1). The number of statements assigned to each
category varied (2–4–8–4–2; 20 statements in total), broadly repre-
senting a bell-shaped distribution, with the middle category having the
greatest number of “slots” to assign statements (Fig. 1). In step 3
(analysis) the researcher analyzes the data using a variety of multivar-
iate data-reduction techniques (such as principal components analysis or
factor analysis) to identify the patterns of Q sorts, representing the main
perspectives or views among the stakeholders (Section 2.2.4). Step 4
(interpretation) presents the results to show how the perspectives are
different or similar and explains the key characteristics of those
perspectives.

2.2.1. Determining the statements for the Q set
Relevant ESs delivered by agricultural landscapes were selected from
the four categories of ESs, i.e., provisioning services, supporting ser-
vices, regulating services and cultural services (Haines-Young and Pot-
schin, 2018; MEA, 2005). We determined 20 relevant ESs of agricultural
landscapes in total and for each ES we formulated one statement
describing it. Hence, our Q sorting results characterize the perceived
importance of different ecosystem services. The statements were
initially formulated based on the original definition of ESs that are
relevant in the context of agricultural landscapes. Before the formal
sorting exercises, three rounds of pilot sorting exercises were done to test
whether the statements and pictures were understandable for local
farmers and minor revisions were made. The final 20 statements related
to 20 ESs (Table 2) were printed onto cards (in Chinese).

2.2.2. Stakeholder identification and selection
Before the survey, we did a stakeholder identification analysis (Reed
et al., 2009) to select the most relevant stakeholder groups in Quzhou
county. Stakeholder groups were considered relevant if they had basic
knowledge about agriculture or potentially had an interest in ESs and
agricultural development, or their actions might impact ESs. The final
list of stakeholders was established after consulting experts and local
informants (Table 3).
We sampled 35 villages across all the 10 townships in Quzhou. The
majority of farmers in the villages are smallholders (Xu et al., 2024).
Five smallholder farmers were randomly chosen from each village to
ensure the survey’s feasibility within a time and budget framework. The
village heads helped identify up to two leader farmers, defined as those
running cooperatives, family farms or having participated in profes-
sional trainings. All 35 village heads and nine township heads were

2.2.3. Conducting Q sorts and getting importance scores
The Q sorts and interviews were done from December 2020 to April
2021 by a pre-trained team of instructors. We asked the farmers and
citizens to sort the cards according to their own opinion, but for other
stakeholders, we asked them to represent the point of view of their
institution or committee.
During the sorting exercise, each participant was asked to arrange
the statements into the grid according to the level of importance of the
statements (Fig. 1). For the respondents who had difficulty reading, the
instructors read the statements out aloud and explained statements with
the help of pictures. In the first round of card-sorting, the participants
initially sorted the cards into three piles for “important”, “not impor-
tant” and “neutral” statements, without a limitation in the number of
statements in each pile. During a second round, the participants were
asked to decide the extreme answers of “very important” and “very
unimportant” statements from the piles of “important” and “unimpor-
tant” services in the first round. The remaining statements were placed
in the three central columns of the grid ranging between important,
neutral and unimportant. The most important and unimportant state-
ments in the Q sorts were then discussed in a post-hoc interview to get a

deeper understanding of the participant’s reasonings.

2.2.4. Determining the perspectives: Q analysis
The data from the Q sorting were analyzed using Q analysis, which is
based on principal component analysis (PCA). Similar to standard PCA,
the data are reduced by Q analysis to a few factors (or principal com-
ponents), which represent the perspectives shared by groups of people.
This analysis serves as a tool to uncover the underlying patterns in how
people fill the Q sorts similarly, thereby highlighting shared ways of
thinking across the group, and revealing the commonalities in how
participants rank the statements in the Q sort. We began with a
comprehensive Q analysis using all 327 Q sorts. Following this, we
conducted separate Q analyses for each of the seven stakeholder groups.
This approach allowed us to identify distinct perspectives within each
stakeholder group and was deemed necessary due to the over-
representation of farmers among the respondents.
For each Q analysis, the number of factors was determined based on
the commonly used statistical criteria like scree plots, explained vari-
ance, the number of participants flagged and the Kaiser-Guttman crite-
rion (Borcard et al., 2011; Cliff, 1988). These criteria typically indicate
the selection of a small number of perspectives. In combination with our
expert judgment, we finalized the number of meaningful perspectives,
which in this case ranged from two to three (Andreotti et al., 2023).
The factor loadings, z scores and factor scores are the key results
from the Q analysis to describe each perspective (Brown, 1980; Zabala
et al., 2018) and the calculation is well established in the ‘qmethod’
package (Zabala, 2014) and other Q analysis software. Factor loadings

2.2.5. Comparing preferences for ESs among perspectives
In this study, the statements represent the ESs and the factors denote
the different perspectives on the relative importance of these ESs. The
factor scores were used to compare the relative importance of individual
ESs across perspectives. We also compared the preferences across ES
categories by calculating the scaled average factor scores for each ES
category. Scaling between 0 and 1 (multiplied by 100, resulting in values
0–100) was needed due to the different number of statements across
categories (2 for provisioning ESs, 4 for supporting ESs and 7 for regu-
lating and cultural ESs). We define a perspective prioritizing certain ES
categories when the scaled average factor scores were higher than 50. A
detailed calculation of scaled average factor scores is provided in the
supplementary material.

2.3. Personal, household and village characteristics

Following the Q sort, farmers were asked to fill in a structured
questionnaire, which consisted of two main parts: 1) crop production
information for each farm, including total planting area and revenues of
crops, etc.; 2) basic information of participants, including age, gender,
education, years of farming, type of farmer (smallholder farmers or
leader farmers), off-farm occupation, family income, etc. Other stake-
holders were interviewed to understand their roles and work experience
and we collected their age, education level and gender. To protect the
privacy of participants, we cannot specify the specific positions of these
respondents in their organizations.

2.4. Statistical analysis

The mean and standard deviation of ES preference scores within
different stakeholder groups were calculated, and visualized using the
“Likert” (Bryer et al., 2016) and “ggplot2” packages (Wickham et al.,
2016) in R (R Core Team, 2021).
To test the difference in ES importance scores among stakeholder
groups, parameter-free Kruskal–Wallis tests were conducted in R. The
confidence level for these tests was 95 %. The mean of each ES impor-
tance score from all the stakeholder groups was calculated by dividing
by the maximum value, 5 (very important), and plotted as the size in
bubble plots by the package of ‘ggplot2’ (Wickham et al., 2016) using R
(R Core Team, 2021).
As farmers were considered a central group of stakeholders, we
aimed to establish which farmer characteristics were associated with
different perspectives. We expected that personal characteristics,
household characteristics and farm characteristics could influence
farmer perspectives (Table 4). Kruskal-Wallis tests were conducted for
continuous variables to determine whether there were significant dif-
ferences among perspectives, and Chi-square tests were conducted for
categorical variables. The confidence interval for these tests was 95 %.

The analyses were conducted in R (R Core Team, 2021).

3. Results

3.1. Overview of the respondents and ecosystem services importance
scores

In total, we obtained 327 completed Q sorts and accompanying in-
terviews. Of the respondents, 13.5 % were females and 86.5 % were
males. Respondents were on average 53.7 years of age and had 9.7 years
of education (around the age of completing junior high school) (Table
S1). More than half of the respondents sorted scientific and educational
value, profit provisioning, water provisioning (irrigation), and air
quality as important ecosystem services (scores 4 or 5) of agricultural
landscapes (Fig. S3). Ecosystem services with low importance (scores 1
or 2) according to more than half of the respondents were the presence
of natural habitats (both within the fields and on field borders), agro-
tourism, climate regulation, and pollination (Fig. S3).

3.2. Different scorings among stakeholder groups

Stakeholder groups were different in their personal characteristics
(Table S1) and their scoring of ecosystem service importance (Table S2
and Fig. 2). Academics, citizens, companies and county governmental
staff rated the importance of biological control on average higher than
farmers, village heads and township heads did. For cultural services like
social relations and spiritual value, citizens had mostly similar impor-
tance scores as farmers, while companies cared relatively less, except for
scientific and educational values. We found that academics rated the
importance of natural habitats in general higher than other respondents.
All the stakeholder groups assigned low importance to climate regula-
tion and pollination, and more importance to profit provisioning, air and
water quality, and water provisioning.

3.3. Different perspectives within stakeholder groups

As a first step, we conducted a single overarching Q analysis with all
327 participants. This analysis identified three main perspectives:
Perspective 1 emphasized the importance of provisioning services;
Perspective 2 focused on regulating and supporting services; and
Perspective 3 highlighted cultural services (see Table S4 and Fig. S6).
This comprehensive Q analysis also illustrated the diversity of per-
spectives within the stakeholder groups (Table S5). However, because

4. Discussion

This study analyzed the perspectives of stakeholders on the impor-
tance of ecosystem services (ESs) provisioning in a case study area in the
North China Plain using Q methodology. It introduces a novel applica-
tion of Q methodology by directly linking the statements to specific ESs,
differentiating our work from traditional approaches. In general, the
participants assigned the highest importance to provisioning services
and the lowest importance to supporting services in this study. We found
most of the respondents regarded scientific and educational value, profit
provisioning, water provisioning (irrigation), and air quality as impor-
tant ecosystem services of agricultural landscapes in Quzhou (Fig. S3 in
Supplementary Material). Notable differences emerged among stake-
holder groups, e.g., academics assigned higher importance to supporting
services while the companies assigned less importance to cultural ser-
vices (Fig. 2). There were different perspectives within each stakeholder
group (Fig. 3), and totally we found 18 different perspectives from 7
different stakeholder groups. While most perspectives showed a com-
bination of preferences for at least two different ES categories, we also
observed similarities across stakeholder groups (Fig. 4 and S7). Five
perspectives mainly prioritized provisioning services, seven perspectives
prioritized regulating services and supporting services, and four per-
spectives prioritized cultural services. This pattern is further confirmed
by the overarching single Q analysis (Fig. S6 in Supplementary Mate-
rial). As the main stakeholder group, farmers’ perspectives were asso-
ciated with personal characteristics (age and education, Fig. 5), and with
farm and household characteristics (farm size and income, Fig. 5).

4.1. Importance of agroecosystem services: Consensus and disagreement

In this study, the perspective of prioritizing agricultural production
(provisioning services) was held by most of the participants, and this
perspective was found in all of the stakeholder groups. Increasing the
profit of crop production and supplying sufficient dietary nutrients are
also local goals for the long term in the policy agenda. Similar to pre-
vious studies, especially those conducted in areas undergoing rapid
economic development, we found that provisioning services were
generally preferred over regulating, cultural and supporting services in
agricultural landscapes (Wang and Zhao, 2023; Zhang et al., 2020).
Moreover, there was a consensus among stakeholder groups and par-
ticipants with different perspectives that irrigation water provisioning,
water and air quality, and science and educational value are important
ESs in Quzhou. The interviewed stakeholders assigned a low importance
to preventing climate warming although they were aware that temper-
ature is increasing (Cheng et al., 2023). Farmers may also not be aware
that agricultural activities and practices could contribute to climate
warming (Madhuri Sharma, 2020) and they stated that they cannot
control how nature changes. A previous study in China found that
farmers have developed adaptive strategies to deal with the impact of
climate change like increasing irrigation (Zhai et al., 2018). It suggests
that they do not want to suffer the damage from climate change, but they
are also passive about preventing climate warming since they consider it
something outside their control.
Pursuing mainly provisioning services may decrease the delivery of
cultural services in agricultural landscapes (Landis, 2017). Prioritization
of cultural services was a minority perspective in this study. People
holding this perspective may face more conflicting interests with the
majority. As a specific example in the North China Plain, tombs in the
agricultural landscape, while hindering mechanical operations, hold
significant cultural value. Removing them for land consolidation would
be resisted by farmers, particularly older farmers. However, the farmers,
whose cultural services are most affected, often have the least power in
decision-making processes (Hanaˇcek and Rodríguez-Labajos, 2018).

Thus, it is important for policy or decision-makers to realize such power
asymmetry among the stakeholders while making decisions for land use
change and planning, instead of only focusing on major perspectives.
Certain patterns reveal ecosystem services demand bundles: e.g.,
urban visitors favored cultural services (e.g., aesthetics, leisure), while
rural farmers preferred provisioning services (e.g., agricultural pro-
duction, timber) (Zoderer et al., 2019). Our study also demonstrated
that people hold different perspectives showing a preference for
different (combinations of) ES categories. Like previous studies, we
found that within the population of farmers, the perspectives towards
ESs were quite diverse (Teixeira et al., 2018). We observed differences in
preferences among stakeholder groups. However, unlike a previous
study (Zhang et al., 2020) where experts prioritized regulating services
and farmers prioritized provisioning services in a typical Karst basin, our
study found both farmers and experts placed greater importance on
provisioning services. The possible reason is that the case study area is a
county that mostly relies on the rural population and agricultural pro-
duction and is located in the main crop production area.
Gender, age, and education have often been found to be important
factors in understanding people’s perceptions of ESs (Lima and Bastos,
2019). In this study, we provided further evidence similar to previous
studies that the farmers who prioritized agroecosystem regulating and
supporting services were younger (Maas et al., 2021), higher-educated
or larger-scale farm farmers (Teixeira et al., 2018). We found no evi-

be tailored to farmers with diverse perspectives. For example, for older
farmers who prioritize traditional provisioning services, it is essential to
focus on improving economic returns from current cropping systems
while preserving cultural traditions. In contrast, younger, more
educated farmers are more likely to support transitioning to ecological
and multifunctional cropping systems and landscapes. This shift requires
targeted professional training in modern agronomic technologies, along
with strong policy support from government bodies and research
institutions.

4.3. Limitations

We attempted in this study to cover different types of stakeholders
(stakeholder groups), however, citizens were underrepresented. Due to
time and budget constraints, citizen sampling might lack precision, not
encompassing the full spatial and typological spectrum. Despite the fe-
male farmers’ involvement in agricultural production, their decision-

making independence is often lower than that of males (Ge et al.,
2023), and some of them did not want to represent their households to
participate in the survey so we interviewed relatively few females in this
study. The perception of female and male farmers could be different, for
example, females tend to assign more importance to regulating services
and sustainability than males (Mponela et al., 2023). Since female per-
spectives in decision-making were often neglected (Rola-Rubzen et al.,
2020), studying the divergent demands of female and male farmers on
the importance of ESs is pivotal in future studies for advancing agri-
cultural sustainability with a more gender-sensitive approach.
Unlike traditional Q methodology with small samples of participants,
our large sample case showed that a large proportion of the farmers were
not assigned to certain perspectives (Fig. 5). The formulation of the
statement did not engage with stakeholders. The elicited perspectives
thus might not cover all possible viewpoints existing in the whole pop-
ulation in reality (Sneegas et al., 2021). Nevertheless, Q methodology
was found to be useful for capturing and understanding the different
perspectives on ESs importance in this study. It stimulated expressing
opinions when people hesitated to speak openly about a certain topic.
Based on our experience, stakeholders’ thoughts were more deliberate
while they participated in Q sorting and they could rank the ESs
importance with gradients.
This study analyzed certain factors influencing people’s preference
for ecosystem services. However, the preferences can be influenced by
many factors other than personal, farm and household characteristics
used in this study. Some intrinsic factors or psychological factors, e.g.,

Quzhou County is a prototypical agricultural production zone within
the North China Plain, where stakeholder perspectives are predomi-
nantly centered on production functionality. This specific focus raises
questions regarding the external validity of our findings, i.e., the ability
to generalize our findings in a study to other locations, times, and par-
ticipants (Roe and Just, 2009). To address this, future research should
extend to other socio-ecological regions, allowing for a comparative
analysis of our results against those from other contexts to better un-
derstand the broader applicability and potential limitations of Q meth-
odology, which could also ensure a more comprehensive understanding
of the preferences for multiple ESs beyond agricultural productivity.
Internal validity is concerned with the believability of the results
(Streiner, 2020). In Q methodology studies, internal validity can refer to
the transparency in selecting statements and participants, following
recommended steps to minimize subjectivity, etc. (Zabala et al., 2018),
which is what we have done in our study. We also conducted several
analyses to probe into the robustness of our results, but we did not
specifically validate the results through additional data collection within
the study area. Future studies could explore ways to further validate the
results of Q methodology. For example, one approach, as suggested by
Webler et al. (2009), involves having participants self-validate the per-
spectives identified after the Q analysis. In that regard, focus group
discussions could be valuable for validating patterns of perspectives
within specific groups, and discussing potential conflicts and areas of
consensus (Cuppen et al., 2010). While such mixed approaches seem
promising for a deeper understanding of the perspectives elicited by Q
methodology, they also pose some practical limitations. First and fore-
most, analyzing the data, constructing the perspectives, and visualizing
the results all take time, so it would almost certainly require a follow-up
meeting at a much later point with the participants. Post-hoc interviews
with participants seem more feasible and may also help to gain deeper
insights into the reasoning behind their perspectives, though it would
not be possible to directly discuss the perspectives with the participants.

4.4. Implications and outlook

Our study contributes to the literature that used Q methodology to
map consensus and dissensus on ES provision. Previous studies have
employed similar techniques to rank the importance of ESs in various
contexts, such as in wetland ecosystems (Mahlalela et al., 2022), coastal
lagoon ecosystems (Sy et al., 2018), wildlife management (Bredin et al.,
2015), but this study gives the first evidence in the context of intensively
managed agricultural landscapes. Methodologically, this study inno-
vated by incorporating a visual approach during Q sorting practices,
which enhances participants’ comprehension of the statements. Addi-
tionally, we adopted a multiple-step approach to compare the perspec-
tives from diverse stakeholder groups, ensuring a comprehensive
understanding of the varying views on the importance of ESs. We
anticipate that these methodological advancements expand the appli-
cability of Q methodology in agricultural science.
It has been well recognized that a “One-size-fits-all” policy would not
meet the different demands for landscape management from stake-
holders. This analysis aims to contribute to integrating the stakeholders’
preferences into future agricultural landscape redesigning and planning
aimed at enhancing multifunctionality. The Q methodology results can
assist the process in two ways: 1) engaging the stakeholders while un-
derstanding the difference of their perspectives and 2) setting clear
prioritized goals based on stakeholders’ perspectives.
Integrating stakeholders’ participation is one of the key elements for
the landscape approach to improve the sustainability in landscapes with
land uses that result in conflicting outcomes (Sayer et al., 2013).
Although the diversity of the perspectives provides a challenge for
policymaking, the results from Q methodology can give a better

comprehension of what the different perspectives are about. Policy-
makers can tailor communication strategies to engage stakeholders with
distinct views effectively (Maas et al., 2021). Coupled with other
possible participatory methods, Q methodology can also be used to
monitor the changes in stakeholders’ demands along the projects’
progress (Andreotti et al., 2023). However, it is necessary for stake-
holders to understand concretely how their decision will impact the
landscape and thus their livelihoods and well-being. The outcomes and
trade-offs of the ecosystem services supply from agricultural landscapes
need to be well-informed for stakeholders in different policy scenarios
(Bernues et al., 2019). We also suggest that future studies complement Q
methodology with deep interviews or focus groups for participants to
self-validate their perspectives.
To co-design farming systems and landscapes beyond economic
goals, diverse stakeholder preferences necessitate additional objectives
(Meynard et al., 2012). With people’s preferences for ESs, decision-
makers could identify the essential action points in the next step to
improve the ESs with higher importance and allocate resources effi-
ciently for maintaining human well-being and agricultural landscape
sustainability (Smith and Sullivan, 2014). Multi-criteria Decision Anal-
ysis (MCDA) techniques could be used to prioritize ecosystem services
based on stakeholders’ preferences, such as Analytic Network Process
(ANP) method (Parra-L´opez et al., 2008; 2009). These techniques can
weigh indicators based on stakeholders’ preferences for ecosystem ser-
vices to translate the multitude of science-based indicators into a more
tangible set of indicators, which may better align with the needs and
perceptions of nonscientist stakeholders such as citizens, resource
managers, and policymakers (Groot and Rossing, 2011). The other way
around, stakeholders’ preferences can also be translated into specific
science-based indicators for long-term monitoring and informing ESs
management. The MCDA methodologies can further be used to calculate
the priorities of each individual ecosystem service based on the scores
from Q methodology in this study. It will be useful for future decision-
making if the preferences for ESs can be linked with measurable in-
dicators or management and policy options or alternatives (Parra-L´opez
et al., 2008). However, it is also worth noting that the multi-criteria
decision-making approach can never give the “right answer” for
decision-making, it is only to inform the decision-making process and to
provide a structured way of thinking (Belton and Stewart, 2002). To
determine the real priorities for enhancing ESs in certain areas, partic-
ipatory decision-making with multiple stakeholders for certain projects
is necessary.

5. Conclusions

This study revealed that while most stakeholders considered provi-
sioning services as the most important, they also recognized the signif-
icance of non-provisioning services such as water quality, air quality,
and scientific and educational value. The perspectives on the relative
importance of ecosystem services (ESs) varied significantly both among
and within stakeholder groups. Generally, stakeholders’ preferences
showed a combination of at least two ES categories, with notable simi-
larities across stakeholder groups. Five perspectives primarily showed
the preferences for provisioning services, seven showed the preferences
for regulating and supporting services, and four showed the preferences
for cultural services. Although the existence of diverse and complex
perspectives makes it challenging to find a unified goal for all stake-
holders, our research helps identify similar perspectives among different
stakeholders, which can be a foundational basis for future collaboration
and setting specific goals.
The findings on the relationship between farmers’ preferences and
characteristics highlight the importance of targeted interventions and
policies that enhance agroecosystem services in line with farmers’
preferences. Specifically, there should be an emphasis on traditional
